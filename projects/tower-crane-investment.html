MSc Coursework — Engineering Economics

Tower Crane Investment Analysis

When should a contractor replace a working crane rather than keep maintaining it?

Project type

MSc coursework — individual study

Role

Financial modelling and analysis

Scope

Equipment-replacement decision under varying interest-rate scenarios

Methods

Payback-period analysis, annual-equivalent analysis, economic service-life assessment, defender–challenger analysis

01

Why this study

Heavy equipment like tower cranes sits at an uncomfortable intersection of engineering and finance: a crane can be technically functional for years past the point where keeping it is still the cheapest option. This study works through that decision formally, rather than by rule of thumb or gut feel about "how old is too old."

02

Method

The analysis modelled a tower-crane replacement decision using a set of standard engineering-economics techniques applied together rather than in isolation:

  • Payback-period analysis - how long it takes a replacement to recover its own cost
  • Annual-equivalent analysis - converting uneven cash flows over an asset's life into a single comparable yearly figure
  • Economic service-life assessment - estimating the point at which an asset's average annual cost is minimised, rather than assuming a fixed depreciation schedule
  • Defender–challenger analysis - the core replacement-economics framework, comparing the cost of keeping the existing crane (the defender) against a new one (the challenger) year by year

Investment alternatives were evaluated under different interest-rate scenarios, since the "right" answer to a replacement question is rarely stable when financing conditions change.

03

Why it matters beyond the exercise

Every one of these techniques is really a way of making a financial assumption explicit enough to argue with, which is the same discipline I now use on real machinery-investment decisions.

The defender–challenger logic in particular reappears constantly in construction: not just for cranes, but for any recurring "repair it again or replace it" decision, including, in a looser sense, decisions about whether to keep patching an existing workflow or invest in a new system. Treating that as a structured financial comparison rather than an intuition call is a habit this coursework project gave me before I needed it professionally.

04

Reflection

Like the software-selection study, this was coursework, and its numbers were built around illustrative scenarios rather than a live fleet decision. What it built was fluency with a way of thinking, reading financial reasoning into a physical asset's life cycle; that now sits underneath how I look at contractual risk and investment exposure in my current role, and underneath why I'm interested in how firms build the business case for digital tools in the first place.
