MSc Coursework: Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis

Agile Project-Management Software Selection

Choosing between six mainstream project-management tools is not really a features question. It is a weighting question.

Project type

MSc coursework: individual study

Role

Criteria design, data collection, decision-matrix modelling, analysis

Scope

Trello, ClickUp, Asana, Jira, Monday.com, Microsoft Planner

Methods

Decision matrix, SAW, TOPSIS, group decision-making, sensitivity analysis

01

Why this study

Project teams choosing a piece of Agile software rarely lack information. Vendor comparison pages, review sites, and colleagues' opinions are all easy to find. What's harder to get right is turning that information into a defensible decision when different stakeholders weight cost, functionality, and usability differently. This study treats software selection as a structured multi-criteria decision problem rather than a features checklist.

02

Method

Six mainstream tools were evaluated: Trello, ClickUp, Asana, Jira, Monday.com, and Microsoft Planner. They were assessed against criteria spanning cost, Agile functionality, integrations, usability, reporting, storage, and implementation requirements. Rather than scoring tools on a single blended impression, the study applied:

  • a structured decision matrix to make each criterion and score explicit
  • the Simple Additive Weighting (SAW) method as a baseline ranking approach
  • TOPSIS (Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution) as a cross-check against a different ranking logic
  • a group-decision framing, to reflect that real procurement decisions involve more than one stakeholder's preferences
  • sensitivity analysis, to see how much the ranking actually depended on the specific weights chosen

03

Why it matters beyond the exercise

The point of running two different ranking methods was not to double-check the arithmetic. it was to see whether the recommended tool held up once the underlying assumptions changed.

That question, how much a conclusion depends on the weights and assumptions behind it, turns out to matter well beyond software selection. It's close to the same discipline needed when a firm evaluates whether a digital investment is actually worth its cost: the ranking is only as trustworthy as the criteria and weights that produced it, and those are usually where the real disagreement lives.

04

Reflection

This was a coursework exercise, not a live procurement decision, and its findings are scoped accordingly. What it left me with is a method, not just a result: a repeatable way to make selection criteria explicit and testable, rather than defaulting to whichever tool is most familiar or most heavily marketed. That habit, separating the criteria from the conclusion and then testing whether the conclusion survives a change in criteria, is one I've carried into how I think about technology decisions at work.
